The circuit · Winter 2026/27

Sella Ronda

The classic ski circuit around the Sella massif

437 kmRuns on this map
128Lifts
5Toboggan runs
1Superski pass

Four passes, four Ladin valleys and one great rock fortress in the middle: the Dolomites' most famous ski circuit, and how to ride it without racing the last lift home.

In the middle of the Dolomites stands the Sella, a near-circular massif of pale cliffs and terraces. Around its base, lifts and pistes link Val Gardena, Alta Badia, Arabba and Val di Fassa in an unbroken ring, crossing four road passes on the way. You can ski the whole loop in a day on one Dolomiti Superski pass without taking your skis off, and you never repeat a run.

Signposted orange clockwise and green anticlockwise, it is a journey more than a challenge: intermediate pistes, long chairlifts, hut lunches and a new face of the Sella at every pass.

The Ring

One massif, four valleys

Dolomiti Superski gives the Sella Ronda (Sellaronda, Sellarunde) as 44 km: 23 km of skiing and 21 km on lifts, taking around six hours. Some valley tourist boards quote about 40 km with roughly 26 km of pistes; figures depend on where you start and what you count. Either way, the loop passes the villages of Selva (Wolkenstein, Sëlva), Colfosco, Corvara, Arabba and Canazei, and stays above 2,000 m for much of the day.

Alta Badia reckons on roughly two hours on lifts (plus queues) and an hour and a half of actual skiing; the rest is traverses, photographs and lunch. Official descriptions rate the circuit medium, for intermediates with some stamina.

No special ticket is needed: the ring crosses several lift companies, so any valid Dolomiti Superski pass, from one day upwards, covers it (prices in ski passes and prices). In 2020 the German portal skiresort.de named the Sellaronda its Best Ski Tour, an award every valley still mentions.

Orange Or Green

Which way round?

The ring is signposted in both directions. Orange signs lead clockwise: from Val Gardena over Passo Gardena to Alta Badia, then Arabba, Pordoi and back over Passo Sella. Green signs lead anticlockwise, the same passes in reverse.

Val Gardena's lift company describes the green circuit as slightly easier, and Dolomiti Superski's own writer prefers it too. The orange direction, though, includes the classic plunge from Porta Vescovo above Arabba and the Pordoi descents, and Cortina's tourist board recommends going clockwise. Many skiers do both on different days: in reverse it feels like a new trip.

Altitudes from valgardena.it, suedtirol.info and fassa.com. Val di Fassa gives Passo Sella as 2,245 m.

Campolongo, the lowest, is a gentle meadow saddle between Corvara (1,568 m) and Arabba. Pordoi is the highest, and from it a cable car climbs to Sass Pordoi at 2,950 m, a terrace on top of the Sella itself. It runs in summer and winter; for 2026/27 Val di Fassa lists the winter service from 22 December, later than the pistes. Its steep off-piste lines are not part of the circuit (see freeride and avalanche safety).

Passo Sella is the most theatrical: the piste threads the City of Boulders (Città dei Sassi), a field of fallen blocks under the Sassolungo (Langkofel), and the Rifugio Salei near the pass is a favourite lunch stop.

You never ski the same run twice, and the Sella is always on the same side of you.

Where To Start

Every valley is a gateway

Because it is a ring, you can join anywhere and finish where you began.

Ways onto the ring

  • Val Gardena. Orange from Selva on the Dantercepies lift (#30); you can also park on Passo Gardena. Green from Plan de Gralba, reached by the Ciampinoi (#29) from Selva or the Saslong lift (#17) from Santa Cristina. From Ortisei (St. Ulrich), cross the Seceda area from lift #2, Ortisei–Furnes.
  • Alta Badia. Corvara and Colfosco sit right on the ring, which is why Alta Badia's tourist board calls itself the ideal starting point.
  • Arabba. Directly on the circuit; Arabba's tourist board also suggests Malga Ciapela below the Marmolada as a start, via Passo Padon.
  • Val di Fassa. From Canazei or Alba via Col dei Rossi, from Campitello by cable car to Col Rodella, or from Pozza through the Buffaure and Ciampac areas (longer).
  • Cortina d'Ampezzo. The Cortina Skyline gondola from Son dei Prade (near Pocol) to the Cinque Torri chairlift, then Lagazuoi and the Armentarola piste into Alta Badia.
  • Kronplatz. Via Alta Badia; for the way home, the last Piculin cable car runs at 16:30 (17:00 from February).

Local tip

Time it backwards

  • Start before 10:00; Val di Fassa suggests leaving by 9:00.
  • Cross your last pass by 15:30. Every valley gives the same advice: after that, a closed lift can strand you in the wrong valley.
  • Slopes close after 17:00, and some links earlier; check lift times on the Dolomiti Superski app or at the stations.
  • From Malga Ciapela, Arabba warns that the Passo Padon lifts may be closed if you are late, making the return to the car difficult.

The Orange Day

Clockwise from Selva, leg by leg

The sequence below follows Val Gardena's official description of the orange circuit. The green route uses the same lifts in reverse order, starting at Plan de Gralba.

Leg 1

Selva → Passo Gardena → Colfosco and Corvara

Dantercepies (#30) · Passo Gardena 2,121 m

Ride the Dantercepies lift out of Selva to the top of Passo Gardena, then ski the long descent to Colfosco in Alta Badia. A worthwhile variant adds the Edelweiss slopes via the Colfosco lift (#46), the Forcelles chairlift (#50) and the Borest (#34) into Corvara: two extra lifts and about 30 minutes.

Leg 2

Corvara → Passo Campolongo → Arabba

Boè (#19) · Passo Campolongo 1,875 m

From Corvara the Boè lift and pistes lead to Passo Campolongo; one more lift and a descent bring you into Arabba, the halfway mark.

Leg 3

Arabba → Porta Vescovo → Passo Pordoi

Porta Vescovo lifts · Passo Pordoi 2,239 m

The Porta Vescovo lifts (#1, or #20 and #21) lift you high above Arabba, facing the Marmolada glacier; the Funifor alone climbs more than 800 m. A couple more lifts take you to Passo Pordoi, then descents run down towards Plan Frataces and the Lupo Bianco area on the Fassa side.

Leg 4

Lupo Bianco → Col Rodella → Passo Sella → Selva

Pradel/Rodella (#155) · Passo Sella 2,218 m

The Pradel/Rodella lift climbs to Col Rodella, and one more chairlift reaches Passo Sella. Ski through the boulder field to Plan de Gralba and the Piz Sella area, then down to Selva. Alta Badia notes that the descent from Piz Sella into Val Gardena is on a somewhat steeper piste.

Finishing elsewhere. For Santa Cristina, take Plan de Gralba (#44) and Piza Pranseies (#22) to Ciampinoi and ski down. For Ortisei, ride the Ronda Express train (#11) to Col Raiser, then lifts #14 and #8 to Seceda and the run home.

Detours

Gran Risa and the Marmolada

From Corvara, the Col Alt lift (#1) starts a detour of about half a dozen lifts to the Gran Risa above La Villa, the steepest piste in Alta Badia and a World Cup giant slalom course (see famous runs). Allow at least an hour, and on the green route be at the top of Passo Gardena before 16:00.

From Arabba, the Porta Vescovo lifts and Passo Padon lead to Malga Ciapela, where three cable cars climb the Marmolada to Punta Rocca at more than 3,000 m. At Serauta station there is a museum of the Great War, and the descent, La Bellunese, runs 12 km back to the valley (see famous runs). It is a half-day in itself.

Did you know?

A ski race by headtorch

The Sellaronda Skimarathon sends teams of two round the ring on touring skis at night, lit by headtorches: about 42 km with some 2,800 m of climbing. It has run for more than 20 years; the 2027 race is on 19 March, starting in Corvara. Pistes and lifts are not affected for daytime skiers.

On A Board

The Sella Ronda for snowboarders

Alta Badia's tourist board says the circuit is perfectly doable on a snowboard, but warns that some flat, gliding sections favour skiers. Carry speed into traverses, ride with a skier who can tow you on a pole, and expect to unstrap a few times. The green direction, rated slightly easier, makes a sensible first lap. Before You Go

Weather, crowds and help

Take care

When the wind blows

  • Lifts can close without warning in strong wind or heavy snow, especially the high links on Pordoi and Sella. Check the Sellaronda status on dolomitisuperski.com or the app before you set off.
  • If a link closes, follow staff instructions; getting home by bus or taxi may cost extra.
  • Fitness counts. Six hours at altitude is tiring; tired legs on the last runs cause falls.
  • Helmets are compulsory on all Italian pistes, and the slope rules apply throughout (see safety and slope rules).

When to go. For 2026/27 the Val Gardena and Val di Fassa lifts run from 4 December 2026 to 4 April 2027. Arabba's tourist board recommends February to March. Start early and lunch late to dodge the queues.

Good to know

Practicalities

  • Pass: any valid Dolomiti Superski pass; buy online for a discount (see ski passes and prices).
  • App: the My Dolomiti app records distance and height gained around the ring.
  • Guided tours: ski schools in every valley run guided Sellaronda days (see ski schools).
