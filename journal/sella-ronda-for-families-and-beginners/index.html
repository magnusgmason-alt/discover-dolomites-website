The Sella Ronda for Families and Beginners: Are You Ready?

5 min read · Updated 27 October 2026

The Sella Ronda is the most famous ski tour in the Dolomites: a loop of lifts and pistes around the Sella massif, through four valleys and over four passes. Many families and newer skiers want to try it. The good news is that it is not as hard as its reputation. The catch is that it is long, and you need to be able to ski red runs comfortably. Here is how to tell if you are ready, and how to plan the day.

What is the Sella Ronda?

The circuit links Val Gardena, Alta Badia, Arabba and Val di Fassa over the Sella, Pordoi, Campolongo and Gardena passes. In total it is about 44 km, of which around 23 km is skiing and the rest is lifts. You can ski it in two directions, both well signposted:

  • Orange: clockwise.
  • Green: anticlockwise.

You only need the Dolomiti Superski pass to ski it. Local area passes do not cover the whole loop.

Is it suitable for beginners?

The Sella Ronda is officially described as suitable for skiers of average ability. In practice that means:

  • You can ski red runs confidently, in control, even when they are busy or icy.
  • You can ski for five to six hours with breaks.
  • You can ride all kinds of lifts, including chairlifts and gondolas.

If you are still building confidence on blue runs, wait. Spend the first days in one area, take a lesson or two, and try the circuit at the end of the week, or next season. There is no shame in that: a stressful Sella Ronda is not a fun one.

Is it good for children?

Children who ski red runs confidently, and who have the stamina for a long day, usually love it. As a rough guide, many families wait until children are about 8 to 10 and have at least a couple of weeks of skiing behind them. Watch for tired legs in the afternoon, when most accidents happen. Plan plenty of breaks and a proper lunch.

Book a guide

Ski schools in every valley run guided Sella Ronda days. A guide keeps you on the right route, knows the quietest runs and gets you back before the lifts close. For families on their first loop, it is the most relaxed way to do it.

Which direction is easier?

There is no official "easy" direction: both include red runs, and both are signposted all the way. The more useful question is where you start. Pick the direction that gets you going quickly from your own valley, and ask your ski school or hotel which way is less busy that week. Above all, make sure you know the way home if you need to cut the loop short.

How long does it take?

Allow about six hours including lift queues and lunch. Fast skiers do it in four, but families should not try to race it. The official advice is to start by 10:00 at the latest, so you are back before the lifts close. If you are behind schedule by early afternoon, use a lift or a bus back rather than rushing.

Tips for the day

  • Start early and start from your own valley.
  • Follow one colour all day: orange or green. The signs are clear.
  • Take a piste map and keep a phone charged.
  • Plan lunch at the halfway point, and book on busy days.
  • Avoid weekends and holiday peaks, when lift queues are longest.
  • Check the weather. Strong wind can close the high lifts on the passes.
  • Snowboarders: expect a few flat sections where you may need to unstrap.

Not ready yet?

Several areas have shorter, easier loops that give the same feeling, such as the Gardena Ronda in Val Gardena. They are a great warm-up for the big circuit. Read our guides to skiing in the Dolomites for beginners and the best family ski areas.

Rules, prices and dates change every season. We checked them in October 2026 against official sources; always confirm with the ski area or operator before you travel.
